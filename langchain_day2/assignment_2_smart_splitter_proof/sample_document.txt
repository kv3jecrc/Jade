The history of artificial intelligence stretches back further than most people realize, with philosophical questions about mechanical reasoning dating to antiquity. However, the modern field was formally founded in 1956 at a workshop held at Dartmouth College, where researchers first coined the term "artificial intelligence" to describe machines capable of tasks that would normally require human intelligence. Early optimism led to bold predictions about human-level machine intelligence arriving within a generation, predictions that proved dramatically premature as researchers underestimated the sheer complexity of tasks like vision, language, and common-sense reasoning that humans perform effortlessly.

Progress in AI has historically moved in cycles of enthusiasm followed by disillusionment, periods now commonly referred to as "AI winters." During these downturns, funding dried up and public interest waned as the gap between promised capabilities and actual results became too large to ignore. The field recovered each time through new techniques: expert systems in the 1980s, statistical machine learning in the 1990s and 2000s, and finally deep learning in the 2010s, which used artificial neural networks with many layers to achieve breakthroughs in image recognition, speech processing, and eventually natural language understanding that far exceeded what earlier rule-based systems could accomplish.

The current era of large language models represents perhaps the most significant shift yet, since these systems are trained on vast quantities of text and can perform a remarkably wide range of tasks without being explicitly programmed for each one individually. This generality is a departure from earlier AI systems, which were typically narrow specialists built for a single purpose such as playing chess or recognizing handwritten digits. Researchers continue to debate how far these techniques can be pushed, and what combination of scale, architecture, and training methodology will be needed to make further progress toward more general and reliable machine intelligence.
